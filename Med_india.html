ERA I
Rajput Age & Early Medieval India
750 – 1206 CE
Who Were the Rajputs?

Rajputs were warrior clans that rose to dominance in north and west India after the decline of the Gupta and post-Gupta polities (Pratiharas, Palas, Rashtrakutas). They followed the kshatriya (warrior) code and legitimised rule through genealogies tracing descent from the Sun (Suryavanshi), the Moon (Chandravanshi), or sacred fire (Agnikula).

  • Tripartite Struggle (8th–10th c.): Three-way contest for control of Kanauj among the Gurjara-Pratiharas, Palas (Bengal), and Rashtrakutas (Deccan).
  • Pratiharas: Capital Kanauj; founder Nagabhata I; greatest ruler Mihira Bhoja; defended India's northwest from Arab incursions.
  • Chahamanas (Chauhans): Capital Ajmer/Delhi; greatest ruler Prithviraj Chauhan III; lost to Muhammad Ghori at Second Tarain (1192).
  • Chandellas: Bundelkhand; capital Khajuraho/Kalinjar; built the famous Khajuraho temples (Lakshmana, Kandariya Mahadeva).
  • Paramaras: Malwa; capital Dhar; ruler Bhoja I — great patron of Sanskrit learning, wrote on architecture and medicine.
  • Gahadavalas: Kanauj/Varanasi; ruler Govindachandra; last major ruler Jaichand defeated by Ghori (1194, Battle of Chandawar).
  • Solankis (Chaulukyas): Gujarat; capital Anhilwara (Patan); built the Rani ki Vav stepwell and Modhera Sun Temple.
  • Kalachuris: Central India (Tripuri); often warred with Chandellas and Paramaras.
Rajput Polity & Society
  • Polity was feudal — no single paramount power; constant inter-clan warfare weakened collective resistance to invasions.
  • Land grants to vassals (samanta system) in return for military service.
  • Strong code of personal honour, chivalry, and the practice of jauhar (self-immolation by women to avoid capture) during sieges.
  • Patronised temple-building extensively — Khajuraho, Modhera, Osian, Mount Abu (Dilwara Jain temples, built under Solanki patronage).
Arab Invasion of Sindh — 712 CE

Muhammad bin Qasim, general of the Umayyad governor of Iraq, defeated Raja Dahir of Sindh in 712 CE at the Battle of Aror, annexing Sindh and southern Punjab — the first permanent Islamic foothold in India.

  • Sindh became part of the Umayyad Caliphate; local Hindus and Buddhists were granted dhimmi (protected subject) status with religious freedom on payment of jizya.
  • Arab rule introduced new agricultural techniques, copper coinage, and was a channel for the transmission of Indian numerals, astronomy, and Sanskrit texts (translated into Arabic) to the Islamic world.
  • The Arab conquest remained confined to Sindh and did not expand further into India at this stage.
  • Principal source: Chach Nama (Persian chronicle of the Sindh conquest).
Mahmud of Ghazni (r. 998–1030)

Ruler of the Turkic Ghaznavid dynasty (Afghanistan); raided India 17 times between 1000–1027 CE, mainly for plunder, not territorial conquest.

  • Most famous raid: Somnath Temple (Gujarat), 1025 CE — temple destroyed, immense wealth looted.
  • Defeated the Hindu Shahi rulers Jaipal and Anandapal of the Punjab–Afghan frontier.
  • Annexed Punjab to the Ghaznavid empire — only lasting territorial gain in India.
  • Patron of letters: court included Al-Biruni (Kitab-ul-Hind / Tahqiq-i-Hind, a study of Indian society, science & religion) and the Persian poet Firdausi (Shahnama).
Muhammad Ghori (r. 1173–1206)

Sultan of the Ghorid dynasty; unlike Mahmud, aimed at permanent conquest and empire-building in India.

  • First Battle of Tarain (1191): Defeated by Prithviraj Chauhan III.
  • Second Battle of Tarain (1192): Decisively defeated Prithviraj — turning point for Muslim rule in north India.
  • Battle of Chandawar (1194): Defeated Jaichand of Kanauj.
  • His slave-general Qutbuddin Aibak consolidated conquests in India and, after Ghori's assassination (1206), founded the Delhi Sultanate.
  • General Bakhtiyar Khilji raided Bihar and Bengal, destroying the Buddhist monastic university of Nalanda (c. 1202–03).
💡
UPSC Tip Distinguish Mahmud (plunder, 17 raids, Somnath, Al-Biruni) from Ghori (conquest, Tarain battles, founded basis of Sultanate). A favourite trap is mixing up which one defeated Prithviraj Chauhan.

Slave (Mamluk) Dynasty — 1206–1290
Qutbuddin Aibak (1206–1210)
  • Former slave-general of Muhammad Ghori; founded the Sultanate after Ghori's death; ruled from Lahore.
  • Began construction of Qutb Minar and Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque (Delhi) and Adhai Din ka Jhonpra (Ajmer).
  • Known as Lakh Baksh ("giver of lakhs") for his generosity; died falling from a horse while playing chaugan (polo).
Iltutmish (1210–1236)
  • Real founder/consolidator of the Sultanate; shifted capital to Delhi; secured recognition from the Caliph of Baghdad (legitimised his rule).
  • Organised the Iqta system (land assignments to nobles in lieu of salary) and the Turkan-i-Chahalgani (Forty, a corps of loyal Turkish slave-nobles).
  • Introduced silver tanka and copper jital — standard Sultanate coinage.
  • Completed the Qutb Minar.
Razia Sultana (1236–1240)
  • India's first and only female ruler of Delhi; daughter of Iltutmish, nominated by him over his sons.
  • Discarded the veil (purdah), dressed like a man at court, and personally led troops.
  • Deposed and killed by the Chalisa (Group of Forty Turkish nobles) who resented her independent rule.
Ghiyasuddin Balban (1266–1287)
  • Broke the power of the Chalisa nobles; introduced the theory of kingship as "shadow of God" (Niyabat-i-Khudai) and rigid court ceremonial (sijda, paibos).
  • Organised a strong, centralised army and a separate military department (Diwan-i-Arz).
  • Successfully defended against Mongol incursions in the northwest.
⚔️ Alauddin Khilji — The Market Reformer (r. 1296–1316)

Came to power after murdering his uncle Jalaluddin Khilji; one of the most powerful and innovative Sultans.

Market Reforms (to maintain a large standing army cheaply)
  • Shahna-i-Mandi: Grain market — prices fixed and regulated by officers.
  • Cloth market and Cattle market — prices similarly controlled.
  • Horse/slave market — separate market for war animals and slaves.
  • Maintained a network of spies (barids and munhiyans) to report on prices and prevent hoarding/cheating.
  • State granaries built to control supply during scarcity.
Military & Administrative Reforms
  • Dagh (branding of horses) and Chehra (descriptive roll of soldiers) — prevented fraud in the army.
  • Repelled Mongol invasions six times under Chagatai leader Qutlugh Khwaja and others.
  • Raised land revenue to 50% of produce; reduced the power of the nobility and curtailed their wealth.
  • Conquered the Deccan and South India through general Malik Kafur — defeated Devagiri (Yadavas), Warangal (Kakatiyas), Dwarasamudra (Hoysalas), and Madurai (Pandyas).
  • Built the Alai Darwaza and founded the city of Siri.
Muhammad bin Tughlaq (r. 1325–1351)

Known for bold but ultimately unsuccessful administrative experiments.

  • Transfer of capital from Delhi to Devagiri, renamed Daulatabad (1327) — later reversed, causing immense hardship.
  • Token currency — introduced copper/brass coins to be valued as silver; collapsed due to large-scale counterfeiting.
  • Khorasan expedition — raised a huge army for a planned (never executed) campaign in Central Asia; drained treasury.
  • Qarachil expedition — failed campaign into the Himalayan foothills.
  • Doubled taxation in the Doab region, triggering famine and revolt.
  • Ibn Battuta (Moroccan traveller) visited his court and served as a qazi; left a detailed account (Rihla).
Firuz Shah Tughlaq (r. 1351–1388)
  • Built the city of Firozabad in Delhi; repaired the Qutb Minar (damaged by earthquake/lightning).
  • Established shifa khanas (hospitals) and a department for finding jobs for the unemployed (Diwan-i-Khairat).
  • Abolished 23 oppressive cesses (abwabs); reduced number of taxes to four sanctioned by Islamic law.
  • First Sultan to levy jaziya on Brahmins (previously exempt).
  • Brought two Ashokan pillars to Delhi (from Topra and Meerut).
  • Dug new canals for irrigation; founded towns Hisar, Jaunpur, Firozpur.
  • Made the Iqta and slave-corps (bandagan) hereditary — weakened central control, hastened later decline.
Decline: Sayyid & Lodi Dynasties (1414–1526)
  • Timur's invasion (1398) — sacked Delhi just before Sayyid rule began, leaving the Sultanate severely weakened.
  • Khizr Khan founded the Sayyid dynasty as a nominal vassal of Timurid rulers; dynasty controlled only a small region around Delhi.
  • Bahlul Lodi (1451) founded the only Afghan dynasty to rule Delhi; expanded into Jaunpur.
  • Sikandar Lodi — able administrator; founded Agra (1504); standardised measurements.
  • Ibrahim Lodi — last Sultan; alienated Afghan nobles, notably Daulat Khan Lodi (Punjab governor) who invited Babur to invade.
  • Sultanate ended at the First Battle of Panipat (1526), where Babur defeated and killed Ibrahim Lodi.
ERA III
Administration of the Delhi Sultanate
Structures of Governance
Central Administration
  • Sultan: Supreme head — military, judicial, executive authority; theoretically subordinate to the Caliph.
  • Wazir: Prime minister, head of revenue department (Diwan-i-Wizarat).
  • Ariz-i-Mumalik: Head of military department (Diwan-i-Arz).
  • Diwan-i-Insha: Department of correspondence/royal letters.
  • Diwan-i-Risalat: Department of religious/judicial affairs, headed by Sadr-us-Sadur.
  • Qazi-ul-Mumalik: Chief justice.
  • Barid-i-Mumalik: Head of state intelligence/news reporting.
Provincial & Local Administration
  • Iqta: Territorial assignment given to a noble (muqti/wali) in lieu of salary, in exchange for military service and revenue collection.
  • Shiq: Sub-division of a province under Sultanate administration.
  • Pargana: Group of villages, administered by Amil (revenue collector) and Patwari (accountant).
  • Village level: Headman (Muqaddam/Khot) and accountant (Patwari) managed local affairs.
  • Land revenue (kharaj) was the chief source of income, generally 1/3 to 1/2 of produce.
Key Administrative & Social Terms
Jizya: Tax on non-Muslims
Zakat: Tax on Muslims (2.5% of wealth)
Khalisa: Crown land
Inam: Land grant, often tax-free
Waqf: Land/endowment for religious-charitable use
Sijda & Paibos: Balban's court ceremonials
Dagh & Chehra: Alauddin's army reforms
Iqta: Revenue assignment to nobles
Karkhana: State-run royal workshop
ERA IV
Vijayanagara & Bahmani Kingdoms
1336 – 1565 CE
Vijayanagara Empire

Founded in 1336 CE by brothers Harihara I and Bukka I (Sangama dynasty) at Hampi on the Tungabhadra river, with the spiritual guidance of saint Vidyaranya.

Four Ruling Dynasties
  • Sangama (1336–1485) — founding dynasty.
  • Saluva (1485–1505) — Saluva Narasimha usurped the throne.
  • Tuluva (1505–1570) — golden age; included Krishnadeva Raya.
  • Aravidu (1570–1646) — final, declining dynasty after Talikota.
Krishnadeva Raya (1509–1529)
  • Greatest Vijayanagara ruler; defeated the Gajapatis of Odisha and the Bahmani successor states.
  • Wrote Amuktamalyada (Telugu) — work on statecraft.
  • Court had the Ashtadiggajas — eight great Telugu literary scholars, including poet Allasani Peddana.
  • Built grand temples (Vitthalswami, Hazara Rama temple at Hampi) and the Krishna temple.
Capital: Hampi (Vijayanagara)
Decline: Battle of Talikota, 1565
Travellers: Nicolo de Conti, Abdur Razzaq, Domingo Paes, Fernao Nuniz
Admin unit: Nayankara system (military chiefs)
Bahmani Kingdom

Founded by Alauddin Bahman Shah (originally Hasan Gangu) in 1347 CE, breaking away from the Delhi Sultanate. Capital initially Gulbarga, later shifted to Bidar.

  • Perennial conflict with Vijayanagara over the fertile Raichur Doab (between Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers).
  • Firuz Shah Bahmani — patron of arts and learning.
  • Mahmud Gawan — celebrated prime minister under Muhammad Shah III; reorganised administration into 8 provinces (tarafs); built a famous Madrasa at Bidar; eventually executed on false charges (1481), accelerating decline.
  • Kingdom fragmented (after 1518) into five Deccan Sultanates: Bijapur (Adil Shahi), Golconda (Qutb Shahi), Ahmadnagar (Nizam Shahi), Bidar (Barid Shahi), Berar (Imad Shahi).
Visitor: Russian traveller Afanasy Nikitin
Famous monument: Gol Gumbaz (Bijapur, later)
🏹
Battle of Talikota / Rakshasi-Tangadi (1565 CE) — Combined Deccan Sultanates (Bijapur, Bidar, Ahmadnagar, Golconda) defeated Vijayanagara ruler Aliya Rama Raya. The city of Hampi was sacked and never fully recovered. This effectively ended Vijayanagara's imperial power, though the Aravidu dynasty nominally continued.
ERA V
Provincial / Regional Kingdoms
14th – 16th Century
Bengal Sultanate

Independent from 1338. Ilyas Shah unified Bengal (1352), founding the Ilyas Shahi dynasty. Hussain Shah (1493–1519) — greatest independent sultan; patronised Bengali literature; saint Chaitanya Mahaprabhu flourished in his reign.

Kashmir Sultanate

Founded by Shah Mir (1339). Zainul Abidin (1420–1470), called "Bud Shah" (Great King) — most enlightened ruler; patronised Sanskrit, Persian, and Kashmiri literature; banned cow slaughter; recalled Hindus who had fled persecution.

Malwa Sultanate

Founded 1401 by Dilawar Khan Ghuri. Hoshang Shah built the Hindola Mahal and Jami Masjid at Mandu (capital, renamed Shadiabad). Annexed by Gujarat Sultanate in 1531.

Gujarat Sultanate

Founded by Zafar Khan (1407). Mahmud Begarha (1458–1511) — most powerful ruler; conquered forts of Champaner and Girnar; capital Ahmedabad; major centre of trade.

Jaunpur Sultanate (Sharqi)

Founded by Malik Sarwar (1394) after Timur's invasion weakened Delhi. Known as the "Shiraz of India" for cultural patronage; Atala Masjid is a notable monument. Annexed by the Lodis (1483–84).

Mewar (Rajput)

Rana Kumbha built Kumbhalgarh Fort and the Vijay Stambha (Tower of Victory) at Chittorgarh, commemorating victory over Mahmud Khilji of Malwa. Rana Sanga nearly unified the Rajputs before being defeated by Babur at the Battle of Khanwa (1527).

Babur (r. 1526–1530) — Founder
  • Descendant of Timur (father's side) and Genghis Khan (mother's side); originally ruler of Farghana (Central Asia).
  • First Battle of Panipat (1526): Defeated Ibrahim Lodi using tulughma (flanking) tactics and gunpowder artillery under Ustad Ali and Mustafa.
  • Battle of Khanwa (1527): Defeated the Rajput confederacy under Rana Sanga of Mewar — secured Mughal hold over north India.
  • Battle of Chanderi (1528) and Battle of Ghaghra (1529) — defeated Afghan chiefs and the Sultan of Bengal.
  • Wrote his memoirs, the Baburnama, in Chagatai Turkish — a vivid account of his life and India.
  • Laid out Persian-style gardens (charbagh) in India, e.g. Ram Bagh, Agra.
Humayun (r. 1530–40, restored 1555–56)
  • Faced rebellion from brothers and the rising power of Sher Shah Suri.
  • Defeated at Chausa (1539) and decisively at Kanauj (1540), forcing him into 15 years of exile.
  • Took refuge with the Safavid Shah of Persia, Tahmasp I, gaining Persian cultural/artistic influence that later shaped Mughal painting.
  • Regained the throne in 1555 after Sher Shah's successors weakened; died in 1556 falling from the steps of his library in Delhi.
🎓 Akbar (r. 1556–1605) — The Great Consolidator
Early Reign & Conquests
  • Ascended at age 13; regency under Bairam Khan who won the Second Battle of Panipat (1556) against Hemu.
  • Conquered Malwa, Gondwana, Chittor & Ranthambore (Rajputana), Gujarat (1572), Bengal (1576), Kashmir (1586), Sindh, Orissa, and parts of the Deccan (Khandesh, Ahmadnagar).
  • Battle of Haldighati (1576): Mughal forces under Man Singh defeated Rana Pratap Singh of Mewar, though Mewar was never fully subjugated.
  • Most Rajput states accepted Mughal suzerainty through alliance & matrimony (e.g., Amber/Jaipur under Bharmal); Mewar remained defiant.
Religious Policy
  • Abolished jaziya (1564) and pilgrimage tax on Hindus.
  • Built the Ibadat Khana (House of Worship, 1575) at Fatehpur Sikri for debates among scholars of different faiths.
  • Din-i-Ilahi (1582): A syncretic ethical order, NOT a new religion — drew from Islam, Hinduism, Zoroastrianism, Jainism, and Christianity. Had only about 18 followers, mostly courtiers; Birbal was the only Hindu to accept it.
  • Policy of Sulh-i-Kul ("peace with all") — religious tolerance as state policy.
Jahangir (r. 1605–1627)
  • Real name Salim; rebelled against Akbar before succeeding him.
  • Married Nur Jahan (Mehrunnisa) in 1611 — she became extremely influential in governance.
  • Famous for the symbolic golden "Chain of Justice" outside his palace for subjects to seek redress.
  • British ambassador Sir Thomas Roe visited his court (1615–18), securing trading rights for the English East India Company.
  • Ordered the execution of Sikh Guru Arjan Dev (1606).
  • Wrote the autobiographical Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri.
  • Patron of naturalistic painting — artist Ustad Mansur famed for depicting flora and fauna.
Shah Jahan (r. 1627–1658)
  • Reign considered the golden age of Mughal architecture.
  • Built the Taj Mahal (1632–53) for wife Mumtaz Mahal, the Red Fort and Jama Masjid in Delhi, and founded the city of Shahjahanabad.
  • Commissioned the ornate Peacock Throne (Takht-e-Taus), later carried off by Nadir Shah in 1739.
  • Launched campaigns into the Deccan against Ahmadnagar and Golconda.
  • Deposed by son Aurangzeb in 1658 amid the war of succession; imprisoned in Agra Fort till his death (1666).
Aurangzeb (r. 1658–1707)
  • Came to power after defeating brothers (notably Dara Shikoh) in a brutal war of succession.
  • Empire reached its largest territorial extent under him, but at immense military and financial cost.
  • Re-imposed jaziya (1679) on non-Muslims, reversing Akbar's policy; banned music at court; destroyed several temples (policy debated by historians).
  • Issued the Fatwa-i-Alamgiri — compendium of Islamic law for administration.
  • Conquered the Deccan Sultanates of Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687), but faced prolonged resistance from the rising Maratha power under Shivaji and later his successors.
  • Spent the last 25+ years of his reign in the Deccan fighting the Marathas — drained the treasury and weakened central control.
  • Death (1707) is widely seen by historians as marking the start of Mughal decline.
ERA VII
Mughal Administration in Depth
Systems Under Akbar & Successors
Mansabdari System

Every officer (mansabdar) held a dual numerical rank: zat (personal pay/status rank) and sawar (number of cavalry the holder was obliged to maintain).

  • Ranks ranged from 10 to 10,000; ranks above 5,000 normally reserved for princes of the blood (some special nobles got up to 7,000–12,000).
  • Mansabdars were paid in cash or, more often, through revenue assignments called jagirs (NOT hereditary, unlike the earlier Iqta in spirit but distinct in practice).
  • Dagh system (horse branding) continued from the Sultanate period to check fraud in maintained cavalry.
  • System became strained under Aurangzeb due to a shortage of jagirs relative to the growing number of mansabdars (the so-called "jagirdari crisis") — a key structural cause of later Mughal decline.
Land Revenue System
  • Todar Mal's Dahsala / Zabt system: Ten-year average of prices and crop yields used to fix cash revenue rates per unit of land.
  • Land measured using the standardised ilahi gaz and a uniform bigha.
  • Land classified into four categories: Polaj (continuously cultivated), Parauti (fallow), Chachar (fallow 3–4 years), Banjar (uncultivated for 5+ years).
  • Revenue typically fixed at 1/3 of the average produce.
  • Khalisa land — revenue went directly to the imperial treasury (not assigned to a jagirdar).
Central & Provincial Offices
  • Wazir / Diwan-i-Kul: Finance minister, head of revenue.
  • Mir Bakshi: Head of the military department, in charge of mansabdars.
  • Sadr-us-Sadur: Head of religious & charitable endowments.
  • Qazi-ul-Quzat: Chief justice.
  • Provinces (Subas): Headed by a Subedar; below him Diwan (revenue), Bakshi (military pay), Qazi (justice), Kotwal (law & order in towns).
  • Akbar organised the empire into 12 provinces (subas); later expanded under successors.
ERA VIII
Later Mughals & Decline
1707 – 1857 CE
Causes of Mughal Decline
  • Aurangzeb's policies: Re-imposition of jaziya, certain temple destructions, and the prolonged, costly Deccan wars alienated Rajputs, Marathas, and drained the treasury.
  • Weak successors: After Aurangzeb (1707), about a dozen emperors ruled in roughly 50 years, mostly as puppets of powerful nobles (the "King-makers").
  • War of succession after every death — no fixed law of succession destabilised the throne repeatedly.
  • Jagirdari & Mansabdari crisis — too many nobles competing for limited revenue-yielding land.
  • Rise of regional/successor states: Marathas, Sikhs, Jats, Rohillas, and semi-independent Nawabs of Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad.
  • Foreign invasions: Nadir Shah (1739) sacked Delhi, massacred citizens, and carried off the Peacock Throne and the Kohinoor diamond. Ahmad Shah Abdali invaded multiple times (1748–1761), including the decisive Third Battle of Panipat (1761) against the Marathas.
  • Growing European commercial & political presence — the English, French, Dutch, and Portuguese trading companies gradually expanded influence amid the political vacuum.
Last effective emperor: Aurangzeb (d. 1707)
Last titular Mughal: Bahadur Shah Zafar (deposed 1857)
Nadir Shah: Sacked Delhi, 1739
ERA IX
Rise of the Marathas
17th – 18th Century
Shivaji Maharaj (1630–1680)
  • Founder of the Maratha kingdom; carved out territory from the Bijapur Sultanate and resisted Mughal expansion.
  • Crowned himself Chhatrapati at Raigad (1674) in an elaborate coronation following Vedic rites.
  • Built/captured a chain of forts (e.g., Raigad, Pratapgad, Sinhagad) using innovative guerrilla tactics (Ganimi Kava).
  • Set up an efficient administration with eight ministers (Ashtapradhan); systematised land revenue.
  • Famous episodes: killing of Afzal Khan (Bijapur general, 1659); escape from Mughal custody at Agra (1666) under Aurangzeb.
Maratha Confederacy & Peshwas
  • After Shivaji's successors, real power shifted to the Peshwa (chief minister), especially under the Bhat family.
  • Balaji Vishwanath and Baji Rao I expanded Maratha influence across much of India.
  • Maratha confederacy included semi-autonomous houses: Scindias (Gwalior), Holkars (Indore), Bhonsles (Nagpur), Gaekwads (Baroda).
  • Third Battle of Panipat (1761): Marathas suffered a heavy defeat against Ahmad Shah Abdali — checked their northward expansion temporarily but they recovered within a decade.

Mughal Painting School
  • Founded by Akbar, who invited Persian master painters Mir Sayyid Ali and Abdus Samad to his court.
  • Major project: illustrated Hamzanama — around 1,400 large paintings on cloth depicting the adventures of Amir Hamza.
  • Jahangir's reign = peak of naturalistic and portrait painting: artists Bichitr, Abul Hasan, Ustad Mansur (famous for studies of birds, animals, and flowers).
  • Shah Jahan patronised refined court and miniature portraits, often static and formal compared to Jahangir's naturalism.
  • Decline under Aurangzeb due to his austere religious outlook; many painters migrated to regional courts (Rajasthani & Pahari schools later flourished).
Sultanate-era Architectural Features
  • Introduction of the true arch and true dome (using radiating voussoirs) — distinct from indigenous corbelled/lintel-beam construction.
  • Use of concrete and mortar as binding material instead of clamps and dowels.
  • Decorative use of calligraphy, geometric and arabesque patterns (avoiding figural representation in religious buildings).
  • Indo-Islamic style is broadly seen in sub-styles: Imperial (Delhi Sultanate), Provincial (Bengal, Gujarat, Jaunpur, Malwa, Deccan), and Mughal.
ERA XI
Language, Literature & Music
Cultural Synthesis
Language Developments
  • Persian: Official language of the Sultanate and Mughal courts.
  • Urdu (Hindavi/Rekhta): Emerged as a composite language from interaction of Persian/Arabic with regional Hindi/Khari Boli dialects, especially in army camps and Sufi circles.
  • Amir Khusrau (13th–14th century) — called "Tuti-i-Hind" (Parrot of India); pioneer of Hindavi poetry; credited (debatably) with inventing or popularising the sitar and tabla; composed riddles, do-sukhane, and the genre of qawwali.
  • Regional languages flourished under Bhakti poets: Hindi (Kabir, Tulsidas), Marathi (Tukaram, Eknath), Bengali (Chaitanya tradition), Punjabi (Guru Nanak), Telugu (Krishnadeva Raya's court).
Important Literary Works
  • Baburnama — Babur (autobiography, Chagatai Turkish).
  • Humayun-nama — Gulbadan Begum (Babur's daughter), on Humayun's reign.
  • Akbarnama & Ain-i-Akbari — Abul Fazl; the Ain is an administrative gazetteer of Akbar's empire.
  • Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri — Jahangir's own memoirs.
  • Padshahnama — official chronicle of Shah Jahan's reign.
  • Amuktamalyada — Krishnadeva Raya (Telugu, statecraft).
  • Ramcharitmanas — Tulsidas (Awadhi, retelling of the Ramayana).
  • Chach Nama — Persian chronicle on the Arab conquest of Sindh.
  • Tahqiq-i-Hind / Kitab-ul-Hind — Al-Biruni's study of India.
  • Rihla — Ibn Battuta's travel account.
Music
  • Tansen — one of the Navaratnas (nine gems) of Akbar's court; composed ragas such as Miyan ki Todi and Miyan ki Malhar; credited with developing the Dhrupad style further.
  • Sufi-influenced qawwali tradition popularised by Amir Khusrau, centred around shrines like that of Nizamuddin Auliya.
  • Aurangzeb's later reign saw an official (though widely disregarded) ban on court music; many musicians moved to regional Rajput and Deccan courts.
ERA XII
Economy, Trade & Society
Medieval Period Overview
Land Revenue — The Backbone of Economy
  • Land revenue formed the primary source of state income — typically 1/3 to 1/2 of produce depending on the period and ruler.
  • Batai/Ghalla-bakshi: Crop-sharing system — actual division of produce between state and cultivator.
  • Kankut: Estimation of expected yield based on inspection of standing crop.
  • Nasaq: Revenue fixed using past records/averages without fresh measurement each year.
  • Zabt (Akbar): Cash assessment based on the Dahsala system (Todar Mal) — ten-year average of prices/yields used to fix per-bigha revenue rates.
Sher Shah Suri's Economic Reforms
  • Built the Grand Trunk Road from Peshawar (Sonargaon end in Bengal) with rest houses (sarais) at intervals.
  • Introduced the silver Rupiya and copper Dam — became the standard for later Mughal currency.
  • Reformed land revenue with patta (deed) and qabuliyat (deed of agreement) documenting cultivator rights and obligations.
  • Reorganised the postal system using a network of horse-relay stations (dak chaukis).
Trade & Ports
  • India exported fine textiles (cotton & silk), indigo, spices, and saltpetre; imported horses, bullion (gold/silver), and luxury items.
  • Key ports: Surat (most important Mughal-era port, called "Gate to Mecca"), Calicut, Masulipatnam, Hooghly, Cambay.
  • Bankers (shroffs/sahukars) facilitated long-distance trade through hundis (negotiable bills of exchange).
  • Karimi merchants dominated the lucrative Red Sea spice trade in earlier centuries.
  • European trading companies (Portuguese from 1498, later Dutch, English, French) progressively established coastal factories from the 16th century onward.
Crafts, Guilds & Industry
  • Textile weaving (khatris, julahas, tantis) was the largest craft industry — Bengal famous for fine muslin ("woven air"), Gujarat for silk and cotton.
  • Akbar established royal karkhanas (state workshops) producing luxury textiles, arms, and decorative items for the court.
  • Metalwork, shipbuilding, paper-making, and indigo processing were significant regional industries.
Social Structure
  • Society remained largely agrarian; caste hierarchy persisted among Hindus, with regional variations.
  • Muslim society had its own social stratification (Ashraf/elite vs. Ajlaf/lower groups), often along ethnic/occupational lines.
  • Sati and jauhar persisted among some Rajput communities, especially during war; purdah practised in elite households of both communities.
  • Position of women varied by class: royal/noble women (e.g., Nur Jahan, Gulbadan Begum, Chand Bibi) wielded real influence; common women faced greater social restriction.

Sufi Orders (Silsilas) in India
  • Chishti: Most popular and widespread in India; deliberately avoided state patronage; key figures — Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti (Ajmer, dargah a major pilgrimage site) and Nizamuddin Auliya (Delhi).
  • Suhrawardi: Generally accepted state office and patronage, unlike the Chishtis.
  • Qadiri: Gained prominence later, associated with figures like Dara Shikoh (Shah Jahan's son), who was sympathetic to Hindu philosophy.
  • Naqshbandi: Stricter, orthodox order; Sheikh Ahmad Sirhindi was a notable critic of Akbar's liberal religious policies and Din-i-Ilahi.
Impact of Bhakti–Sufi Movements
  • Promoted ideas of social equality — challenged caste rigidity, untouchability, and empty ritualism.
  • Encouraged the growth of vernacular literature — Hindi, Marathi, Punjabi, Bengali, Assamese — making religious and ethical ideas accessible to ordinary people.
  • Fostered Hindu–Muslim cultural synthesis — shared shrines, common devotional practices, syncretic art and music forms.
  • The Guru Granth Sahib (Sikh scripture) incorporates verses of Bhakti saints like Kabir, Farid, Namdev, and Raidas alongside the Sikh Gurus' own compositions — a direct textual marker of this synthesis.
